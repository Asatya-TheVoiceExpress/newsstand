The First Dictator

A re-examination of Julius Caesar beyond Shakespeare's romantic portrayal — the general who conquered the Gallic tribes by exploiting their internal divisions, triggered a civil war, and earned enough enemies to be stabbed twenty-three times by his own colleagues.

By Mouli Sharma
19 September 2023 · 3 min read

Julius Caesar and His Many Jeopardies

I first came across the name, Julius Caesar, while flitting through the pages of my middle school textbook three quarters of a decade ago. The great Roman ruler, romanticized in the words of Shakespeare, painted a somewhat sympathetic picture in my mind. A man slayed by his own; how could one not feel pity? I was young and impressionable, taking William's affinity for drama to be mortal truth. Julius therefore did enjoy a high position (heh) in my mind for a couple of years. Now that I am older, I feel obligated to hold myself accountable.

A mere 5 years into the rule, he was assassinated with a dagger (& then several more) through the heart by those he trusted most. And the question that both thespians & newsreaders of 44 BC have deigned to haunt us with forever, is did he deserve it?

What comes to mind when hearing the word 'Dictator' is normatively defined. Cruel, evil, merciless - the worst person ever. But its etymology is fairly neutral. It derives, obviously, from the term 'dictate,' i.e. 'to tell someone what to do.' A dictator as a political role then, in essence, is little more than managerial. Its only association to popular history is that the exemplary work of dictators like Stalin, Hitler, and Kim Jong-Un has managed to paint a prevailing mindset that dictators are inherently awful and malevolent individuals, and that much is fine. But then you see Mustafa Kemal, fondly known as Atatürk - ' father of the Turks, ' a revolutionary nationalist who rose against the Ottoman Empire in the early 20th century and led Turkey on a path of modernization. You see Lee Kuan Yew, who led Singapore to independence from British rule in 1959 and elevated its agricultural economy to new heights, laying the foundation for Singapore to become one of Asia's wealthiest countries. You see more & you read more & you're forced to ask - what gives?

The modern Geo-political world has afforded too much importance to words and labels. A young girl who likes video games becomes an NLOG becomes a Pick-Me. A terrorist becomes a Prime Minister becomes a Gold Medalist becomes a World Leader. A CEO becomes a Genius becomes Revolutionary becomes a Meme. Invaders become Natives become Civilized become Developed. The Indian state of Allahabad becomes the Bharatiya state of Prayagraj. It seems that nouns, even proper ones, now carry the potential to alter the course of history.
