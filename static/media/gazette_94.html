Women's College Infirmary Charges Five Rupees Per Pad; Automatic Dispensers Charge Ten

At Kalindi College — a Delhi University institution that calls itself all-women's and teaches courses in feminist theory — the medical infirmary charges students five rupees per sanitary pad. A ground report on the gap between institutional pride and the institutional care actually on offer.

By Mouli Sharma
29 March 2024 · 8 min read

Excessive control & a resource-precious attitude make Kalindi College's medical infirmary functionally redundant.

February 25, 2023, New Delhi: On 8 March 1914, the arrest of Sylvia Pankhurst was the domino that tipped the way to the celebration of International Women’s Day today. Over a century later, things are little more than marginally better, and the consolation we get is that at least they're 'changing.' And slowly but surely, it seems they are. Urban women’s lives have lost all identifiable traces of the barbaric pasts that unfortunately still cling to many. Yet there are some fundamental structures of our society, the very institutions that were meant to be the flag bearers of this change - lead the way, so to speak - that are perhaps holding it back. In Delhi University's Kalindi College, one that takes great pride in calling itself an all-women’s institution (one that boasts of being one of what are DU's handful of institutions that have a media department, & in it countless courses has countless readings on gender, society & feminist theory), an individual sanitary napkin at the already ill-equipped & resource-fond infirmary incurs students a cost of a loud, dumbfounding 5 rupees. Decisions like these are what make the Pink Tax so pervasive & inescapable in modern societal structures. The average ranges of Whisper & Stayfree napkins, two of India’s most popular brands & also the most commonly stocked in colleges like Kalindi, are priced at rupees Rs. 26-29 for the one-week packs, i.e. Rs. 4 per pad. Charging students a single rupee more, therefore neither subsidises this cost for them nor makes any significant contribution towards recovering let alone profiting off of it. The only purpose it serves, is to discourage students from using a facility that is meant to be for them.

A ‘Well-Equipped’ Room: Shell Facilities in Government Institutes

“My friend was on her period the other day, and so I'd gone to grab her a pad from the infirmary. I was charged 5 rupees for the napkin. The incident was stupefying in a way. It is not just about the amount you have to pay but the fact that you have to pay at all,” says Shruti Patel, a senior at Kalindi & Head of Design at TVE. “You don't expect there to be a fee on a pad in a college 'medical room.’” “It's degrading,” added another student. Interestingly, this resource-precious attitude is not limited to sanitary napkins, but extends to the entire facility of what Kalindi College's website describes as a ‘well-equipped medical room [where] the college provides good medical facility to students & staff.’ Students are forced to purchase basic first aid items like band-aids & gauze from pharmacies outside the campus or exaggerate difficulties to be provided any help at the infirmary. “I remember my ankles being scraped raw the day of our NCC fest. I went to the medical room hoping to get some gauze or anything for relief, but all they gave me was one band-aid. The nurse said she wouldn’t give me even a second one unless I showed her blood,” says Manasvi Vats, the JUO of Udaan, Kalindi College’s NCC. Manasvi tells us she had to call up her friend to bring her band-aids from home instead. They cost 2 rupees. Dr. Rajshree Lal of the medical staff remains available for attending students and staff for their ‘minor complaints’ for three hours every alternate day of the week. The remaining time, the room is under the management of Ms. Rekha, who visits the college every day from 9 AM to 5 PM. Most students know the medical room as synonymous with nurse Rekha, a smiling, kind-faced woman who would rather the cleaning staff do her dirty work for her, both literally & otherwise, as the following account from a senior who would rather remain anonymous describes. “I remember having a fever of around 100 one time back in my first year. I somehow managed to drag myself to the medical room in between classes - & this was nearly noon, so the heat was severe - & it was closed. The door was bolted. I looked around, I tried to ask people, but there was no one & I was doing so poorly that eventually, I couldn’t wait anymore. I thought I’d at least get inside, and sit down; I was on the verge of passing out. So I unbolted the door & sat down & there was so much immediate relief, getting off my feet & out of the heat. I must have waited for - what, at least another 15 minutes, before anyone came. I was so happy I thought I’d finally get something, anything, even just a Crocin, some damn electrolytes, whatever they’d give me, at that point, I’d be grateful. But turns out it was just someone from the cleaning staff who’d noticed the door was open. She came in & started interrogating me, chastising me for presuming to let myself in, implying I may have vandalised the room & god knows what else. She stayed till the nurse arrived, which was another 15 minutes later, but she made it very clear it wasn’t out of any real concern for my well-being, just ‘keeping watch’. By the time the nurse arrived, I was already sufficiently distracted from the fever by the third degree. She silently watched as the woman berated me for another long while, not just condoning but appreciating what had happened before finally asking me what was wrong. She gave me a small paracetamol tablet & made me sign in the register. I’ve never gone to the medical room since.” The authorities’ description of the room & its capabilities make it clear that they do not exceed beyond being able to provide extremely elementary medical aid to students for their, in their own words, ‘minor inconveniences.’ So, the refusal of medical staff to then entertain & treat what inconveniences they do deem as minor, beg the question what purpose, if any, this facility serves at all. Accounts like the ones above are reflected in the tired resignation found in the two demographics most well versed with the medical room, NCC & Sports. “We used to train (march) out in the parking lot during the summers & students would pass out or come close on a near daily basis. If you bring another student to speak for you, the nurse reluctantly gives you glucose or something, because they can exaggerate the issue. If you are, say, dehydrated to the point of blacking out, you can get a glass of glucose,” said a senior of Udaan. This is especially egregious in the face of the faculty’s revelation that the only qualified doctor on the premises is only there to treat minor medical issues, transforming these incidents from those of general apathy to legitimate medical malpractice, constantly baiting students to bring them more than they can chew, & refusing to bite what they can. What is most concerning about these incidents is they don’t seem to exist in isolation, but instead stem from a larger culture of institutional incompetence. Aspects of this incompetence can be observed in most government institutions, & Delhi University is no exception, but Kalindi College’s sanitation sees a version of it so extreme that it stands in significant contrast to even other colleges of DU. Indira Gandhi Institute of Physical Education & Sports Sciences is a small, single-department college in Delhi’s Vikaspuri. It is one of the 12 colleges of Delhi University 100% funded by the Delhi Government instead of the centre, & its labs, faculty & academia all suffer from a lack of regular financing. Even here, toilets are clean, & pads are free, as told by Ritu, a first student in the only program offered by the college. So it is not a question of budget, but a question of apathy & intent. Kalindi College, infamous for its mismanagement & regular controversies recently went viral on Instagram due to the abysmal state of its student toilets. Its academic contributions are a long & undulating list of predatory journals & frivolous conventions. It must then be observed that the seeming redundancy of the infirmary is not noteworthy because it is anomalous, but precisely because it isn’t; following along a disturbing pattern of mismanagement at an institute that, despite its A+ NAAC accreditation, is plagued by a lack of seriousness.
