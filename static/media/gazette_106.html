“Have Spoken Ill of Him in Life, Will Continue to Do So in Death” – Leftist Youth Group Slams ‘Posthumous Veneration’ of Ratan Tata by Foreign & National Press

As politicians and press commemorated Ratan Tata's death with state funeral proposals and Bharat Ratna nominations, COLLECTIVE Delhi released a statement refusing to observe the silence demanded by mourning — recounting the Tata Group's documented history of forceful land acquisition and labour suppression that his philanthropic image was built to obscure.

By Mouli Sharma
12 October 2024 · 18 min read

COLLECTIVE Delhi Secretary Sourya’s statement recounts the Tata Group’s long history of forceful land acquisition & human rights violations, calling out the selective memory & revisionist narrative of Tata’s posthumous virtue being put forth by the media in the wake of his death.

As VIPs spent the last 48 hours celebrating the life & mourning the loss of Tata – who died of natural causes on Wednesday morning at the age of 86 – to immense media attention, one side of the coin that has been wholistically ignored by mainstream as well as large parts of independent media is that of the Tata Group’s political history, one that is fraught with violence and controversy. In the cacophony of mournful obituaries & funeral telecasts 2 drowning out any and all counternarratives from the newssphere, one demographic that is sustaining a quiet, consistent voice of dissent, is the youth. Whether through student representatives or through digital apparatuses 3 , the young left has held onto its views on Tata, intent on highlighting the group’s bloody history.

As the country’s press covers which celebrities were seen in attendance of Tata’s (Twitter) funeral, which 23 of his quotes make the best compilation, & writes a headline for every person – from a newspaper vendor 4 to Amitabh Bachchan – that can offer a quote of condolences for the tycoon, young journalists, digital media influencers, & left-wing student organisations in their infancy have been unflinching in their continued critique of the less picturesque aspects of the Tata Group’s evolution. This is the first of a series of articles that hopes to uphold, even if only microscopically, the desperate & delicate balance of news that the Indian press today has seemed to forsake. Here’s charting the other side of the coin.

As in life, so in death.

Left, Right & Centre: Was Tata Beloved By All?

“Today, the 'leftist' Chief Minister of Kerala and a billionaire from Gujarat are both paying tribute to Ratan Tata. It is a matter of great pride for any individual when ideological opponents come together in mourning, but this reflects something deeper.” — Sourya, General Secretary, COLLECTIVE Delhi

This dichotomy, between right, left & Tata, is not an isolated & interesting case study of human multitudes, but something that has remained ever-present throughout the partly fabled tale of the Tata Group’s 200-year 8 tumultuous history. Tata’s institutions & architectures have long defined the murky space of the Indian political centre, having been founded as a business empire that formed the very blueprint of industry in a mixed economy. As India’s economy has shifted away from the part-socialist model to an increasingly capitalist one, the conglomerate’s constitution has similarly evolved. Yet even in 2024, in the steel rods that hold up this country & in the military vehicles that surveil it, the dichotomy survives.

Bastar, Tata Steel, & The ‘Biggest Land Grab Since Columbus’

“On one hand, to ideologically defend their profit-based system, Tata Steel has established institutions like the Tata Institute of Social Sciences (TISS) and Tata Environment Research Institute (TERI) using their financial clout; on the other hand, to pit the oppressed against each other, private militias like Salwa Judum and Operation Green Hunt have been funded by houses like Tata and Jindal.” — Sourya, General Secretary, COLLECTIVE Delhi
“The memorial for 13 martyrs in Kalinga Nagar, Odisha, remains a proud symbol of tribal resistance [against Tata Steel]. When the country’s first ‘Special Economic Zone’ was established in Bengal during the CPI(M) regime in 2003, Tata [was among] its first consumers. In Singur and Nandigram, landless and small farmers were forcibly evicted from their land [by the government for Tata’s Nano plant]. In 2015, when demands for a union arose against falling real wages, union leadership was illegally ousted. [...] Tea plantation workers who grow Tata Tea are now facing the police to unite in Darjeeling. From Kashmir to Manipur and Nagaland, Tata trucks and drones are merely another name for military rule.” — Sourya, General Secretary, COLLECTIVE Delhi

Censorship, Palestine, & Institutional ideology

The comparison to Jindal above holds true for Tata in another, even more damning sense. While both groups hide their investment in militant violence & unlawful land acquisition – an ordinary procedure in the industries of steel & power – behind philanthropy & founding educational institutes, the flagship institutions of both groups have come under fire in the past year for controversies relating to censorship of scholars & student groups 15 . While Jindal’s offices issued ‘warnings’ to faculty in the academic sphere for speaking on the escalating violence in Palestine, TISS went as far as formally banning a leftist student organisation, the Progressive Student’s Forum 16 .

“This notice addresses a critical issue concerning the presence of an unauthorised and illegal forum on our campuses, reportedly the ‘PSF TISS’. This group has been engaging in activities that obstruct the institute’s functions, defame the institute, demean members of our community, and create divisions among students and faculty. It has been observed that this group is misleading, distracting and misguiding students from their academic pursuit and harmonious life in the campus. (sic)” — Order by the Office of Registrar, Anil Sutar, Tata Institute of Social Sciences, 19th August 2024 (as reported by Hindustan Times )

Such orders have been on the rise all across India’s higher education institutes, a large number of whom – as rightly being pointed out by independent creators like Dhruv Rathee & Ravish Kumar – have been quietly founded by the Tata Group all along, even those that don’t have ‘Tata’ in the name 17 . Certainly, if the higher educational institutes founded by corporations serve as representations of their & their leader’s thoughts, Tata has time and again made clear where it stands, against the left, if not quite all the way on the right 18 .

But institutional ideology & administrative decisions come in handy for little more than speculation regarding their leaders’ philosophies. A much stronger tool for silent diplomacy in modern geopolitics, is trade 19 , and when it comes to issues like the Palestinian Genocide, the Tata Group has taken an extremely explicit political stance with its supply of Range Rovers to the Israeli Military for use in the Occupied West Bank 20 .

In the Murky Middle: Revisionist History & PR

Readers at this point in the article, have they not been keeping up with the news, may find themselves confused with this statement. After all, when there is genocide – several counts thereof – on one’s resume, what ambiguity can there be?

On the one hand, Tata Steel’s MoU with the state of Chattisgarh involved the signing of a Rs. 10,000 cr. contract for tribal land that would be acquired through the mass killing of tribals by government-employed child soldiers 23 , but it was not Ratan Tata who hired them. It was not Ratan Tata who fired the gun. It is not Rata Tata who pushed employees into vats of steel on a semi-regular basis in his factories or gave speeches against unionisation & worker’s rights. In 2015, the BJP government in Chhattisgarh revoked Tata’s licence 24 over the land it stole from Bastar due to its continued delay in building the steel plant that it had all been for. What is the ideology that one should extract here?

It is this necessitated speculation that breeds ambiguity. There is no ‘source’ telling us which of these decisions were his & what his stances were regarding each one, & it is possible there never will be. Tata’s love for dogs & concern for the middle class on the other hand is verifiably his own. In his own voice & word, conveniently amplified by the media, is the image of a man who ‘donated himself out of billionaire lists’ & flew economy by choice. It is no wonder that people from every strata & ideology queued outside his funeral to get one last glimpse of Ratan Tata’s enshrined body; it is the only look at the man they ever got.

Effective Altruism & Funeral Politics: The Ethics of Philanthropy

Ravish Kumar’s 24-minute bulletin in tribute to Ratan Tata highlights his firmly pacifist stance during the 26/11 attacks on the Tata Group-owned Taj Hotel in an atmosphere of rising anti-Pakistan & anti-Muslim sentiment. As Sourya points out in his statement, silence in times of polarisation is in itself a statement of complicity, & Tata’s silence at the time was antagonistic to the Indian right-wing, spearheaded by the Bajrang Dal & RSS – who were later accused of having orchestrated the attacks after the assassination of Hemant Karkare 25 .

One of the main defences being employed by the present press narrative exalting Tata is his notable lack of billionaire status. Times Now, Business Standard, India TV & Firstpost each 26 released an article on 10th October 2024 on how despite the multibillion-dollar empire he built in the time he oversaw the family conglomerate, Ratan Tata never made it to a billionaire’s list.

“How is it possible that someone who has been at the helm of the country's largest business conglomerate for six decades (sic) is not in the list of the top 10 or top 20 richest individuals in the country? Yet, this is a fact. The reason for this can also be the large-scale philanthropic work done by the Tata family through the Tata Trust. In fact, the Tata family members traditionally hold minimal personal stakes in their own companies. Jamsetji Tata, the founder of the Tata Group, established a guiding principle that much of the profits earned by Tata Sons should be donated to the Tata Trust for philanthropic purposes. The Tata family has been a pioneer in philanthropic work much before people like Bill Gates.” —Arushi Jaiswal, 10th October 2024, India TV, “Ratan Tata, who built a million-dollar empire, never made it to any billionaires list”

‘Effective altruism’ in the name emerged as a fringe neo-philosophical movement in 2009, & has since undergone a dramatic rise & fall in both popularity & credibility after Sam Bankman Fried – one of the movement’s central figures – was accused and later convicted of crypto fraud last year ; but the philosophy itself isn’t exactly new. Its disgraced founder, 28 William McAskill, merely algorithmised & gave a name to what has always been the main tenet of all philanthropic ethic: In direct opposition to the popular proverb from the Hippocratic Oath, the very idea of ‘earning-to-give’ is premised upon first causing harm, then offsetting it via donation. In her video on effective altruism, YouTuber and thespian Abigail Thorn explains this phenomenon:

“Several years ago I was invited to be the ethics consultant at a banking conference. They put me on a business class flight to a luxury hotel in Madrid, [where] over several days, I and the other attendees discussed how we could fix the various problems facing the world: healthcare, debt, financial collapse. [...] Conferences like that are held all the time. At the top end you've got stuff like the World Economic Forum, the Clinton Global Initiative and the Social Good Summit; further down the list is the thing that I went to. Rich people have been trying to figure out the most effective way to get rid of their money for a long time.” — Abigail Thorn, 25th February 2023, The Rich Have Their Own Ethics: Effective Altruism & the Crypto Crash (ft. F1nn5ter) , @PhilosophyTube on YouTube

The video goes on to trace the downfall of the effective altruism movement, which by 2015, coming back to Tata, had itself grown into a multibillion dollar project . Finishing her anecdote about the banking conference she was invited to, Thorn says, “[When the last day finally] came, I closed out the conference by giving my keynote speech. And my ethical advice to all these bankers was that they should immediately resign.”

Conclusion

“It is said that one should not criticise a person after their death. Well, even during Ratan Tata’s lifetime, we have spoken ill of him and will continue to do so; a life of luxury and comfort tends to reflect a certain soft sensitivity and generosity on a person’s face. A hungry child, writhing with hunger, may grab his brother’s collar and say, “Give me food, you bastard.” It takes centuries and billions of wealth for newly emerged capitalists, who flee their society after violence, to achieve such an innocent image.” — Sourya, General Secretary, COLLECTIVE Delhi

Funerals, after all, are for the living.
